# Copywriting Method

**Summary**: The persuasion-craft playbook for all P&G outward copy — distilled from the Sam Parr (founder, The Hustle) "Dangerously Good Writer" masterclass and the classic ad/copy canon it draws on. Where [[png-voice]] defines *what the voice is*, this page is *how to write persuasively inside it*: headlines, open loops, the slippery slope, story, rhythm, objection-handling, desire-around-price, and the practice method (**copywork**) that builds the skill. Plain-voice reference; the example copy is shown in the ganas voice.

**Type**: concept

## The core idea
- **Copy isn't writing words — it's understanding what people want and getting them to act.** "Getting what's in my head into yours and influencing your behavior." (source: Sam Parr.)
- **The goal is NOT 100% of readers.** It's to get the *right* person to read the *whole* thing — and a large share of them eventually buy. So write for the buyer, not the crowd.
- **Long converts better — if it's interesting.** "There's no such thing as too long, only too boring." Ugly converts; long converts; *boring* loses. (A 2,000-word sales page can convert ~5%.) Don't trim for length — trim for boredom.
- **The secret is constraint → clarity.** Force every sentence to earn its place. Hemingway's power is what he leaves out.

## 1 — Headlines (80% of the work)
- **Ogilvy:** ~5× as many people read the headline as the body — "when you've written your headline, you've spent 80 cents of your dollar." (source: Sam Parr / Ogilvy.)
- **AIDA** — Attention → Interest → Desire → Action. The headline's only job is **attention**.
- **"Restaurant-owner energy"** — talk to the reader like you already know them ("Hey, good to see you again, how's the family?"), not stiff/corporate. High warmth, high energy. *(This is exactly the [[png-voice]] "neighbor who happens to be a pro."*)
- **Targeting / "niches make riches"** — a headline that speaks to *one specific* person pulls harder. "50 things only Denver people know" appeals to few but converts ~30% of them. → P&G: write the tent headline for **the caterer**, the **quinceañera mom**, the **food-truck owner** — not "everyone."
- **Spin the well-known** — twist a known phrase so it sticks ("You may get lost — but not in the crowd"). Use phrasing frameworks (alliteration, the rule of three: "we shall fight on the beaches…").
- **Punch the first line.** The first word/sentence has to land a punch. *"My friends think I'm smart. I'm not. I just read The Hustle."*

## 2 — The open loop / curiosity gap / the slippery slope
- **Mind the gap.** Open a loop the reader *must* close. "They laughed when I sat down at the piano — but then I started to play." Humans can't leave a cliffhanger. In short copy, **stack open loop on open loop**.
- **The slippery slope (Joe Sugarman):** the only job of sentence 1 is to get sentence 2 read; sentence 2's job is sentence 3; and so on. Make the copy frictionless to keep reading.
- **The rule of consistency (Cialdini):** small yes → bigger yes. Get the reader nodding **early** ("Don't you always notice you wake up at the same time every night?" → "yes") and the close gets easier. (The lawn-sign study: a small sticker first lifts big-sign agreement ~40%.)
- → **P&G:** open the email/DM with a line they have to answer ("Got an event coming up?"), get an early yes, then carry them to "send your logo."

## 3 — Everything is a story (customer = hero, brand = guide)
- **Make it a story** — beginning, middle, end, with tension. Stories are how you make "long" *interesting* (and they don't live in a Meta ad library — a moat in the AI age). (source: Sam Parr.)
- **Hero's journey / StoryBrand:** the **customer is the hero**, the **brand is the guide.** The WSJ "Two Young Men" ad (two grads, one read the WSJ and succeeded) ran **28 years / $2B**. P&G is the guide that gets the *entrepreneur* (the hero) to their event looking like a pro.
- **Failed-solution / absolve the guilt:** "You tried to fix this and it didn't work — **that's not your fault**, you just didn't have the right [ingredient/printer/proof]." Removes blame, builds trust.
- **Trojan-horse the product** — bury the product *deep* in the story; build desire first. A bad ad throws the product at you immediately ("our restorative sleep drink"); a good one earns the want before naming it.

## 4 — Rhythm + the rules of writing
- **Good writing has rhythm — like music.** Vary length: *short, medium, long, short.* Most people ramble in the same-length sentence over and over.
- **Write at a 7th-grade level.** Run copy through **hemingwayapp.com**. (USA Today ≈ 4th grade; NYT ≈ 7th.)
- **One sentence = one point.** If you'd use a comma, use a **period** instead.
- **Start sentences with And / But** for rhythm and momentum.
- **Buffett's tell:** he explains complex things in simple sentences — his letters average **~17 words/sentence** (down from 25 over the years). Aim short.
- **Kill your darlings.** Write the first draft, then **cut a third, cut a third, cut a third again.** "Write with your eraser." "Write drunk, edit sober." "I'm a bad writer — a great editor."
- → This *is* the [[png-voice]] **anti-AI rule** in craft form: short, punchy, rhythmic, ruthless trimming. AI copy reads same-length and over-written; this is the antidote.

## 5 — Handle objections inside the copy
- **Pre-empt the objection out loud** (the *8 Mile* move — say the bad thing yourself first so it can't be used against you). "I know what you're thinking — *who is this, why trust them?*"
- **Answer with a personal anecdote, not a claim** — the $40 headphones ad: instead of "waterproof," *"I accidentally wore them in the shower — totally fine."* Shows, doesn't assert.
- Objections can also live in the **FAQ** block (P&G PDPs already use AEO FAQ blocks — good home for "is it really 5 days?", "what if my file's low-res?").

## 6 — Build desire around the price
- Old Harley ads sold **desire**, not "how much you save by pre-qualifying." Put the price *in* the sentence but frame it around the want; the price reads as almost a **bonus**.
- **"Experiences make you happier than things"** — a justification the buyer can tell themselves. → P&G: the tent isn't a cost, it's *showing up like a pro at every event this season.*

## 7 — Make it visceral
- Translate abstract facts into a **physical, shocking image**: a brick of fat = one large popcorn; the deconstructed Coke (40% sugar); *"there's more E. coli in year-old Tupperware than your toilet bowl."* → P&G: don't say "fades in the sun" — say "a banner that looks tired by your second event." Show, don't state.

## 8 — Humor / personality (if it's on-brand)
- Humor is one of the easiest ways to sell **when it fits the brand** (The Hustle welcome email, NY Post headlines, Hampton's about-us page). P&G's ganas voice is warm + human → light humor fits; corporate stiffness doesn't.

## The practice method — COPYWORK (the highest-leverage takeaway)
**To get dangerously good, copy great writing by hand.** Find copy/writing regarded as great and **transcribe it word-for-word, by hand, ~1 hour a day, for 3–6 months** ("copy hour"). You absorb the rules, texture, and patterns the way you learn an instrument by copying songs — then you combine what you like. Physical writing beats typing for retention; *feel* the sentences. Sam's first 2M-view video came after two days of copywork. (source: Sam Parr.)
- **For P&G / Venus / Carlos:** keep a swipe file of great ads + emails; hand-copy a few each week. Start with the canon below + the best P&G emails that *closed*. This is how the team's copy gets sharper over time — and the cheapest skill investment there is.
