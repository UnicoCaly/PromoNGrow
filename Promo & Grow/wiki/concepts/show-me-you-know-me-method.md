# Show Me You Know Me (SMYKM) Method

**Summary**: A cold-email method from Apollo Academy taught by Sam McKenna (founder of SamSales Consulting): every email proves deep, specific research on the recipient so it reads as 1:1 and stands out in an executive's inbox. Five parts — subject line, first sentence, value proposition, the close, and a quality-over-quantity practice cadence. Cited results: industry average ~6% open / 0.9% reply vs SMYKM ~43% open / ~20% reply (source: apollo-smykm-cold-email-course-transcript.md). This page distills the method, captures the two famous subject-line examples, embeds the full transcript, and maps each part to Venus's PNG outbound work.

## Method summary — the five parts

A short note before the parts: the **whole method is one idea** — prove you actually know this specific person, so the email cannot have been sent to anyone else. That signal is what earns the open and the reply. Everything below is in service of that signal.

### 1. Subject line — the most important part

- It's the most important part of the email; you have a few seconds to capture the executive's attention (source: apollo-smykm-cold-email-course-transcript.md).
- **It should read as nonsense to anyone except the recipient.** If a stranger can't decode it, the personalization is doing its job — it signals you researched *them* specifically.
- **Use `+` signs to break up phrases.** Shorter is NOT necessarily better — you do not have to stay under four words.
- **Reuse a clever pattern across people in the same shared context** (e.g. everyone from the same fraternity) while still looking 1:1 — this is how the approach scales without losing the personal feel.
- **Cited results:** industry average ~6% open / 0.9% reply vs SMYKM ~43% open / ~20% reply.

**PNG application** (LA event-services prospects — caterers, event planners, party-rental cos, breweries): the shared-context "nonsense" line is the unlock for a local list. Build subject lines from a real, verifiable detail tied to *that* prospect, joined with `+`:
- **Caterer:** `[their signature dish or recent event] + step-and-repeat + [their company]`
- **Event planner:** `[the venue they just posted] + branded tent + [their company]`
- **Party-rental co:** `[a specific rental in their catalog] + table covers + [their company]`
- **Brewery:** `[their flagship beer or the fest they vended] + feather flags + [their company]`
- The **reusable-pattern-across-shared-context** move maps cleanly to PNG segments: one pattern per segment (e.g. all breweries get `[their beer] + [fest] + [their name]`), each still decodable only by that recipient. Note this is a *deliberate counterpoint* to PNG's current cold rule of "3–4 words, lowercase, human" ([[cold-email-sequence]] / [[venus-email-writing-engine]]) — see the PNG application section for how to hold both.

### 2. First sentence

- **Subject line + first sentence = the preview text**, and the preview text is primarily what decides whether the email gets read (source: apollo-smykm-cold-email-course-transcript.md).
- **Two openers Sam recommends:**
  - **(i) "We have yet to be properly introduced, but I'm [name]…"** — paired with a subject line that proves research, this signals two things at once: we *should* have already been introduced, and you already know something about them. (Sam's go-to at SamSales.)
  - **(ii) Jump straight into the "show me you know me" research hook** — reference something real (e.g. their recent post about their dog), then tie it authentically back to yourself. Executives often post about their dogs; referencing the animal and connecting it to your own opens the door.

**PNG application:** opener (i) works in PNG's warm/bilingual register — "We haven't been properly introduced, but I'm Venus at Promo & Grow…" / "Aún no nos han presentado, pero le habla Venus de Promo & Grow…". Opener (ii) is the stronger play for event-services prospects, who post constantly: reference the actual event they just executed or the fest they vended, then tie it to PNG's display work ("saw [business] crushed the [event] this weekend — that step-and-repeat behind the bar is exactly what we make"). The authentic tie-back is the wedge to the free proof.

### 3. Value proposition

- **Your value proposition is NOT what you do** — most emails describe what the sender does, which is a *marketing* email. The value proposition is **the specific challenge you solve for that particular buyer** (source: apollo-smykm-cold-email-course-transcript.md).
- **Longer is better here** (a deliberate counterpoint to "write short, short, short"): you have to convince the buyer their time with you will be well spent.
- **Pre-empt the buyer's most likely objection inside the first email and rebut it.** State the specific thing you solve, then anticipate the next objection ("we don't need that" / "we already do that") and answer it before they can raise it. Sam's worked example: lead with a specific stat ("your Sales Navigator usage hovers around 5% of licenses — we can bring that up"), anticipate the objection ("we have a CSM at LinkedIn"), and rebut it succinctly in the same email ("our team teaches you to *sell* with the platform the way she did as a LinkedIn executive").

**PNG application:** the challenge PNG solves for event-services buyers is *showing up branded and professional, on time, without design risk*. The likely objections to pre-empt by segment:
- **"We already have a printer / a guy"** → rebut with the free proof + 5-business-days-from-approval wedge ("most shops make you pay before you see it — you see a free proof with your logo first").
- **"Too expensive / we go cheap"** → rebut with premium-not-cheap positioning and the proof stack ([[png-voice]]).
- **"No time before the event"** → rebut with the 5-business-day promise from proof approval ([[cold-email-sequence]]).
- **"We don't need custom"** → rebut with the specific look it gives their setup vs. generic rentals.
- This is the one place SMYKM's "longer is better" most usefully stretches PNG's current "under ~90 words" rule — see how to hold both, below.

### 4. The close

- **Never use a calendar link** — even on warm/referral inbound. It's presumptuous, a little rude, puts the onus on them to schedule, and makes them work around *your* calendar instead of you working around theirs (source: apollo-smykm-cold-email-course-transcript.md).
- **Don't ask "do you have 15–20 minutes tomorrow?"** (too presumptuous on timeline) **and don't propose a specific slot** like "would Monday at 1pm work?" (too specific — it lets them say no to that one slot and leaves you chasing).
- **Instead:** "Do you have time over the next week or two to learn more? Let me know what works for you and I'll send a calendar invite accordingly." — almost nobody writes this, so it stands out, AND it keeps timing in your control (they propose, you schedule, you can rearrange to accommodate them).

**PNG application:** PNG's close is already assumptive-toward-the-free-proof ("reply and I'll start your free proof"), which is even lower-friction than asking for time — keep that as the primary CTA. The SMYKM close is the upgrade for when a *call* is the right next step (a bigger account, a custom/institutional job routed to Carlos): swap any "got 15 minutes?" or proposed slot for "do you have time over the next week or two — tell me what works and I'll send the invite." Reinforces the no-calendar-link rule already in [[venus-email-writing-engine]] / [[cold-reply-playbook]].

### 5. Practice / cadence

- **Quality over quantity.** Set a realistic goal — Sam suggests ~20 well-crafted emails per week — and practice the script until results show (source: apollo-smykm-cold-email-course-transcript.md).

**PNG application:** ~20 deeply-researched SMYKM emails/week is a *complement* to, not a replacement for, the machine-sent [[cold-email-sequence]] volume. The high-research SMYKM lane is best aimed at the **highest-value prospects** — institutional accounts, big event planners, breweries with multi-location reach — where 20 hand-built emails out-earn 200 templated ones. Venus drafts these in the [[venus-email-writing-engine]] and works the replies with [[cold-reply-playbook]].

## The two subject-line examples from the video

**1. Chief Logistics Officer of Tory Burch** (a multi-billion-dollar company):
> "[Russian phrase — not transcribed] — nothing shall ever tear us asunder (except detention fees)"

- "detention fees" is a **logistics in-joke** (a charge in freight/shipping) — decodable only by someone in that role.
- The exec **replied within ~10 hours** of the first email, saying it "stood out in a sea of emails" they get every day.
- This is also the **scale example:** the same pattern could be reused on other executives from that same fraternity while still looking 1:1.
- *(The Russian/Cyrillic phrase was spoken in the video but not transcribed by the user.)*

**2. Sam McKenna herself** (the line that would work on her):
> "Switzerland + Le Dip cheeseburger + [your company name]"

- Works because: she's **originally from Switzerland** (born just outside Geneva); she **lives in Washington, D.C.**, and waxes poetic about the **"cheeseburger à la diplomate"** ("Le Dip"); and "[your company name]" means nothing to her on its own.
- The first two references make no sense to anyone but her — so they get her to open the email to see what the sender knows about her.

## PNG application

How this connects to Venus's outbound work:

1. **SMYKM is the high-end complement to PNG's machine cadence.** [[cold-email-sequence]] is the templated, segment-homogeneous, machine-sent engine (volume); SMYKM is the hand-built, deeply-researched lane for the **highest-value prospects** (institutional accounts, anchor event planners/breweries). Venus runs ~20 SMYKM emails/week at the top of the list; the machine handles the rest.

2. **Holding the two rule-sets together (the honest tension).** PNG's current cold rules say *short* subject (3–4 words, lowercase, human), *short* body (under ~90 words), assumptive-to-the-free-proof CTA. SMYKM says *longer can be better* for both subject and value prop. These aren't a contradiction — they're **two tiers**:
   - **Tier 1 (machine / volume):** keep PNG's existing short rules ([[venus-email-writing-engine]] checklist) — fast to produce, fine for templated segment sends.
   - **Tier 2 (SMYKM / hand-built for VIP targets):** allow a longer, research-led subject (`detail + product + company`) and a longer value prop that pre-empts one objection. Reserve the time cost for prospects worth it.
   - Both still obey the non-negotiables: **Touch 1 = no link**, **no calendar link**, **"5 business days from proof approval" (never "guaranteed")**, **bilingual only with taste** ([[png-voice]]).

3. **Three SMYKM moves to adopt now, regardless of tier:**
   - **The "show me you know me" first sentence** — reference the real event/post/venue, tie it back to PNG. This raises reply rates on warm inbound too ([[cold-reply-playbook]]).
   - **The no-calendar-link close** — already PNG policy; SMYKM gives the exact better-phrasing ("time over the next week or two… I'll send the invite") for when a call beats a proof.
   - **Pre-empt one objection in the first email** — name the segment's likeliest "no" and rebut it inside the message.

4. **Lane:** Claude-admin builds the SMYKM prompt variant into [[venus-email-writing-engine]] on request; Venus (Carlos/VA approval for any VIP/institutional target) runs the ~20/week and works replies.

### Chapter 1: Intro

The industry average open rate for emails is alarmingly around 6%. And if you want to be even more terrified, their average reply rate is 0.9%, not even a full percentage point. But when you compare that to Show Me, You Know Me open rates, here's what we look like. An average open of 43 percent and an average reply rate of 20%. That's incredible. And it's unheard of in the sales world, which is unfortunate, but also not so unfortunate. Because what that means for you is there's a massive opportunity for you to stand out in executives' inboxes simply by putting some thought into what you write. Let me show you what an executive's inbox usually looks like. It's not pretty, right? "Available to talk." "Can I make an intro." "quick introductions." "quick question." There's so many subject lines that are all generic. They say personalization at scale, and they secretly say, psst, I want to sell you something. You can do so much better than this, and you've got to do better than this if you expect anyone to open, read, and reply to your email. Now let's get into exactly how you're going to do this. The subject line is the most important part of your email.

### Chapter 2: Cold Email Subject Line

This is where you have just a few seconds to capture the attention of the CEO, VP, or whatever executive you're reaching out to. The subject line could look something like this. Now, when you read the subject line, I want you to again reflect, does this subject line make any sense to you? If you say no, then we did our job correctly. This email was written specifically for the chief logistics officer of Tory Burch, a multi billion dollar company. And here's what happened. In less than 10 hours of that very first email being sent, the person responded and they said, your email stood out in a sea of emails that I get every single day. Now, here's a really cool thing about this subject line as well. This is where you can start to scale this. What other executives do you have that were in that same fraternity? Think about how this is a great opportunity to use that same subject line and to use it on multiple executives while still making it look like it was just for them. A couple of other tips. Think about how to break up your subject line. Shorter is not necessarily better. You don't have to adhere to less than four words, and you can use plus signs as a great way to break up the verbiage in your subject line. Referencing those exact tips, here's an example of a subject line that would work on me. Switzerland, plus Le Dip cheeseburger, plus your company name. I want you to think about this subject line. Again, it makes absolutely no sense to you, and it makes absolutely no sense to anybody that you know, except for me. I'm originally from Switzerland. I was born just outside Geneva. I live in Washington, D.C., and I often wax poetic about the cheeseburger a la diplomat. And your company name doesn't make any sense to me, but the first two things do. So it's at least going to get me to open up the email and see what it is that you have to say about the incredible show me you know me that you did on me.

### Chapter 3: The First Sentence in Your Cold Email

The second most important part of your email is the first sentence. Your subject line plus your first sentence is what's known as the preview text, and that's primarily what determines if your email gets read. There are two different approaches that I'd recommend trying here. At my company, Sam Sales, our go to first sentence is this. "Hi, Bill. We have yet to be properly introduced, but I'm Sam McKenna, and…" Now, a couple things here. A lot of people will say not to introduce yourself in the first sentence. It's a waste of space, they say. But here's where I differ in opinion. When you put together a subject line that shows that person that you know them, and you've done research, and you pair it with "we have yet to be properly introduced," what you're signaling to somebody is that perhaps we should have been introduced, and that you do know something about them. Now, another example is to go right into the show me you know me. Don't waste any time. You might say something about their dog in the subject line, and then say something like this in your first sentence. "Bill, I saw your recent post about your adorable furry companion Sprinkles," and then tie it back to your own dog. You'll see some of the most high level executives often post about their dog, so a great way to open up the door with them is to reference those animals, and again, to think about an authentic tie right back to you.

### Chapter 4: The Value Proposition in Your Cold Email

Your value proposition is not what you do. Most emails that we see say what the person does. Now that's a marketing email. So your value proposition is truly the challenge that you solve. What is it that you do for that particular buyer and how can you help them? I see this kind of value proposition all the time. "Saw you're hiring. We see most CROs struggle because they ignore this challenge. Want to hear more?" Now we see emails like this because we're often encouraged as sellers to write short, short, short emails. But again, I beg to differ here. Longer is better, and here's why. We have to convince our buyer that time with us will be well spent. When you state something specifically that you can solve for them, then think, what is the next big objection? What is the buyer thinking? And what's their rebuttal? You stating that you can solve a challenge for them isn't enough. You might get them to either object, say "we don't need that," or say "we already do that." So we've got to get around that objection to begin with. Let's take a look at an example that someone on the SAM sales team might send to a prospect. Let's say our value proposition starts with this. "If you're anything like our clients, you see your usage rates of LinkedIn Sales Navigator hover around 5 percent of your licenses. We can train your teams and bring that number up." I also know that my most common objection is "we have a customer success manager at LinkedIn." So if they said that to me, or if they responded to me in that way, what would I say back? This is what I want to write succinctly in the first email. "Our team will teach you how to use this platform to sell in the same way that she did when she was an executive at LinkedIn for her teams." So what that does for that buyer is, huh, oh, so maybe there is something not only that Sam can do to improve our rates, but to change how we use the platform. And finally, what you thought would never come, the end of the email. How do we close out? For starters, with any outbound email — and I would tell you even referral emails where you get introductions that are inbound — do not use a calendar link. Why don't we want to do this? It's presumptuous, it's a little rude, and it puts the onus on the individual to schedule time with you. Here's the other thing that I don't like about it. Now that individual is working around your calendar instead of you working around theirs. Another thing to avoid is writing something along the lines of, "do you have 15 to 20 minutes tomorrow to chat?" Or "would Monday at 1 p.m. work?" The reason for this is it's presumptuous in terms of a short timeline (tomorrow), or it gets too specific and it allows the buyer to say no to Monday at one. And then you're left in a pattern of chasing them. What I would tell you to do instead is something that looks different, and that looks like this. Simply say, "do you have time over the next week or two to learn more? Let me know what works for you and I'll send a calendar invite along accordingly." Nobody sends scripting that looks like that. So not only will you be different, but you'll take their timing and their calendar into effect, allowing you to schedule the call, you to control the timing, and even for you to rearrange things on your calendar to accommodate them.
